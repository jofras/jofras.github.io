Network Portal

A full-stack network monitoring app for the canton of Neuchâtel.

The network portal project was all about helping our team's engineers with configuration and diagnostics. They were spending so much time weekly, daily even, trying to figure out where customer problems were coming from.

To figure out paths to endpoints, all they had were inventory and database tables, but no easy way of figuring out where stuff connected. They'd have to sort through UUID-keyed link tables, use traceroutes, and then manually query equipment command-line to get info like VRFs and VLANs.

The switch visualizer was created to help on-site technicians. Before, a technician would arrive at a remote site, see a switch full of cables, and then have to call into the office to get an administrator to tell them what was where. The technician was helpless without the administrator, who usually had better stuff to do anyway.

Design

The design process was quite organic for this project. I started with a rough idea of everyone's requirements, then iterated on them, asking the team to use the tool and note what they liked and would like added or improved. This way, over the course of two months, we got the tool into production shape before my contract ended.

Backend

To fix the problem, I started backend. I adapted the total network scanning architecture I had put in place a few weeks earlier to optimize the BFS-style full network scan. Because path tracing and port visualization are fundamentally different operations, I had to implement two algorithms.

The path tracing algorithm, dubbed "macscanner", queries the database with the target IP's address, then joins ARP and subnet tables to get the original router's name. It then runs a recursive next-hop-style algorithm, keeping track of each equipment (and metadata) on the way, until it gets to the endpoint.

The switch visualizer backend, put simply, just queries the switch in question with a bunch of OIDs (what network devices use to store info) and organizes them into a dictionary for the frontend to parse.

Pathfinder Frontend

The pathfinder frontend was kept fairly simple to give users the info they needed as quickly as possible. The tool immediately shows VLAN, VRF, and target IP/MAC — information essential when troubleshooting an endpoint connection. The layout is clear and easily scannable.

Below that, it shows the path through the network, from router to endpoint. The path is a chain of "info-cards" for each piece of equipment, connected by links that show configuration notes and ports at each end. This makes it incredibly easy for engineers to scroll through the path to the endpoint and then backtrack through the network from there.

The design is complete with colors and emojis to make the tool more user-friendly. In my opinion, network infrastructure is already complicated enough, so it's important to have a tool that makes the topic approachable. There is logic behind the colors: cards are outlined based on their category (router, switch, host, etc.), and emojis represent the type of equipment you're looking at.

Switch Visualization

In terms of UI, the switch visualizer was a much more significant project. My boss and I wanted to create something as natural to the task as possible. What would make technicians go "whoa"? Make their job as easy as possible?

So we made the tool a mirror image of the interface they faced in real life: the front of the switch, all ports visible. The technician should immediately know what's connected where.

The biggest requirement was being able to hover over a port and get info on what VLAN was configured and whether the connection was up or not. This inspired using colors per VLAN, and adding small green/red lines to port overlays to indicate operating status.

If something is connected on one of the port's VLANs, the respective VLAN color becomes more opaque.

Another important feature was to add stacks. In networks, you can stack multiple physical switches into one virtual one with more ports. Such switches share an IP address and are managed by a "master" switch. If a technician looks up a stack, all members are returned, complete with chassis info to differentiate between them.

Next, I integrated a table of protocol-discoverable (CDP) neighboring equipment. All of these are visible on some port; on hover/click the corresponding port lights up.

An extra feature I integrated was maps. Once you have the view, you can open the switch's location either in Google Maps, or, using Swiss coordinates, on a custom cantonal map that includes links between equipment.

Once I had all that done, I started finetuning the site with technicians and engineers. We added a MAC search bar (shows you what port a MAC address is connected on), an ALL/UP/DOWN filter view relating to port operational stati, and clickable VLANs (also filter ports).

Later, I added a "Suggest Disconnect" button that, given a VLAN, finds the port configured with that VLAN that's been down for the longest time. It models the technician's most common use case: needing to disconnect a cable to branch a new one, but having no idea which cable to pull.

And finally, we augmented each port with in- and outgoing packet discard and error statistics. This gives the user significant context when troubleshooting: if a customer calls in with a faulty laptop and you see two laptops behind one port and a lot of discarded packets, you can be pretty sure they've installed a small switch that can't handle the traffic.

General UX

The final step consisted of gluing these tools together into one centralized platform to be used across the organization. And having a little fun while doing it.

I created a simple central portal website. Initially, it had a black background and a very straightforward interface: enter an IP address, select a tool, and you're off.

At some point, I thought the black background was kinda boring. It was riding the line of looking like every other IT network tool. So I decided to add a background and a little dragon emoji — which we determined was the canton's new mascot.

I then wanted to add a bit of wonder — a little more intrigue. Inspired by the "Disney-like" quality I had asked the image generation tool to recreate for the background, I inlined some JavaScript to add little glowing yellow lights, fireflies. They moved around the screen slowly, oscillating their glow. It made the portal seem a bit more magical.

Inspired by my initial firefly design, we built seasonal effects: new backgrounds of the city in different seasons, complete with special effects per time of year. Snowflakes in winter, rain in spring, buzzing bees (inspired by a local hockey club's logo) in summer, and falling leaves in autumn.

The pink sky in fall is inspired by my views of the mountains' silhouettes on morning runs by the lake. When it's fall and not foggy, Neuchâtel is beautiful.

I created loading screens with animations to play after launching a tool. They run whenever you change screens and really calmed me down when waiting for a long network scan.

I also added intuitive error views that give users an indication of what's going wrong backend, so that (should a tool fail) they can troubleshoot efficiently.

Next, my boss asked whether I could implement multiple language support, and make the default language French. So with the team's help and some DeepL, I created French, Portuguese and Swiss German translations.

I realized that engineers would often switch between views dynamically: use the pathfinder to check the path, memorize the last switch in the chain, then query the switch visualizer with that IP. So I linked the two tools — switches in the pathfinder now have direct links to their port visualization, and the visualizer links out to neighboring equipment.

Special Days

Admittedly, I could have ended it there. But I had a few days left in the internship, the tool was ready to launch, documentation written, ready to pass the baton to the next intern. I had completed everything that was planned.

But the team there was great, and I wanted to give them a tribute. So for each of the engineers in my office, I created special views, set to show on their birthdays. Inspired by those alternate spellings of "Google" on Halloween or Christmas.

One's themed after Yayoi Kusama for an engineer whose wife and he really like her work — all polka dots and a floating pumpkin with a colorful sparkle effect. Another commemorates the time we went for really good Thai food in the old city. And one for my birthday, a little stamp on the work.

I think it's really important to make tools human. Build an efficient, clean backend, talk to people to understand their needs, integrate them of course. But then add a personal touch, some heartfelt warmth. The world of tech is dehumanizing enough.

Results

So far, the tool has received very positive feedback from team members across the organization it was shown to. To release it for general use, I added authentication based on LDAP and Active Directory.

Apparently, the networks team will send it to clients too, hoping to eliminate some of their calls regarding switch problems, so that engineers can work on their main projects.

Exact estimates of time savings haven't been made, but both troubleshooting on client calls and connecting to switches is sure to move from double-digit minutes to seconds.

I'm grateful to have been given so much autonomy with this project, and happy to have helped the team out by giving some time back. I hope they enjoy the custom views.
